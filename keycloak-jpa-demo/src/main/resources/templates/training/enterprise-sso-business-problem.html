01 · The real problem

One company. Many applications. Too many identities.

Consider a large enterprise operating HR, procurement, finance, supply chain, manufacturing, analytics, and customer platforms. Some are custom Java applications, others are commercial SaaS products. Each might historically maintain its own user database and passwords.

Password proliferation

Employees juggle independent logins, password rules, and reset processes across applications.

Orphaned accounts

A departing employee can remain active in a system when its local account isn't disabled promptly.

Fragmented oversight

Security teams struggle to apply consistent MFA and sign-in policies or reconstruct access decisions.

Operational overhead

Application administrators repeat onboarding, access resets, and offboarding across disconnected systems.

02 · The human story

A new employee's first week—and last day

Follow a fictional employee, Maya, through everyday events. SSO is not just a convenience feature; it is one piece of identity lifecycle control.

Day 1: Maya joins Finance

HR records Maya as an employee. IT provisions a corporate identity. Without integration, each application team may need a separate account request.

Day 2: Maya needs four applications

She opens payroll, expenses, procurement, and an internal Spring Boot portal. Independent login systems mean inconsistent MFA, passwords, and support workflows.

Month 6: Maya changes department

New responsibilities require additional access; old privileges should be reviewed and removed. SSO alone does not solve authorization or role lifecycle management.

Last day: Maya leaves

Central identity disablement can prevent fresh federated sign-ins at participating applications, but existing sessions, local accounts, and non-SSO access paths also require explicit controls.

The business question

Can an organization establish one authoritative sign-in decision across independently built applications, while each application retains appropriate control over what the user is authorized to do?

Three separate responsibilities

Identity lifecycle: Who is this user, and should their account exist?

Authentication: Has a trusted identity provider authenticated them?

Authorization: What can this authenticated user do in this specific application?

04 · The architecture, in business terms

A trust chain—not a shared password database

Your course uses a real-world integration pattern: a Spring Boot application uses OpenID Connect with Keycloak. Keycloak brokers authentication to Microsoft Entra ID using SAML 2.0. Each party has a distinct responsibility.

Employee + BrowserWants access to business application
Spring Boot + Spring SecurityProtects application endpoints, initiates OIDC login
Keycloak 26OIDC provider to Spring; SAML broker to Entra
Microsoft Entra IDEnterprise SAML IdP authenticates workforce user

The critical protocol distinction

Spring Boot ↔ Keycloak: OAuth 2.0 / OpenID Connect (OIDC), often Authorization Code with PKCE where configured.

Keycloak ↔ Microsoft Entra: SAML 2.0 identity federation carried through browser redirects and form POSTs.

Business benefit: the application stays insulated from the external enterprise IdP's SAML details while Keycloak bridges the identity technologies.

SSO does not mean...

  • All applications share one user database.
  • All apps grant identical privileges.
  • Disabling a user instantly revokes every established session.
  • A valid SAML signature alone guarantees correct authorization.
  • Provisioning, account linking, and JIT creation happen automatically.

06 · Enterprise decision scenario

A SaaS vendor asks to bypass corporate SSO. Is that just a login preference?

Consider a procurement SaaS containing supplier banking details, confidential contracts, and approvals. Its administrators suggest switching employees to a local password login because federation troubleshooting is inconvenient.

Short-term appearance

Users might reach the application by entering separate vendor-managed passwords. The immediate SSO issue seems resolved.

But what needs review?

  • Will corporate MFA and conditional access still apply?
  • How are terminated employees blocked?
  • Who owns password reset and compromised-account response?
  • How are authentication and privileged actions audited?
  • Which vendor-supported identity options exist?

Enterprise-level decision

Evaluate alternate access methods as a security, operations, and governance change—not just a UX workaround.

Run a controlled incident review; test federation in lower environments; establish break-glass access under strict controls; document session revocation, ownership, and approval requirements.

Takeaway: An authentication change can alter the organization's ability to govern employee access well beyond the login screen.

Keycloak can act as a protocol bridge: SAML federation upstream, OIDC to the application downstream. Both protocols are relevant, but they solve different integration boundaries here.

What the audience should remember

Enterprise SSO is a business control implemented through technology.

The goal is not simply one-click login. It is trusted enterprise authentication across independent applications, with clearer ownership, governed identities, controlled access, operational resilience, and traceable security decisions. That is why SAML 2.0, Microsoft Entra ID, Keycloak, Spring Security, and custom authentication flows matter together.
